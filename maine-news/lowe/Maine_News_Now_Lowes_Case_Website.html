Special Report • Reardon v. Lowe's

The Lowe's Case That Wouldn't Go Away

A self-represented Maine plaintiff says a Lowe's employee injured his hand. Lowe's tried to end the case before trial. The federal court said no. Now the company's pretrial strategy includes an effort to use the plaintiff's criminal convictions for impeachment.
Based on public court filings in Nathan Reardon v. Lowe's Home Centers, LLC, U.S. District Court for the District of Maine, Case No. 1:25-cv-00099-JAW.

Nathan Reardon alleges that he was injured in the checkout area of the Lowe's store in Brewer, Maine, in December 2019 when a cashier mishandled a wooden fence post. According to the court record, the post fell toward Reardon, he reached to catch or deflect it, and it struck his thumb. Lowe's disputes negligence, medical causation, and damages.

Unlike many customers facing a major corporation, Reardon is not represented by a law firm. Lowe's is represented by experienced outside counsel from Morrison Mahoney LLP. Yet the case has survived Lowe's attempt to dispose of it before a jury could hear the evidence.

The key fact is not that Reardon has already won. He has not. The key fact is that Lowe's asked the court to end the case before trial — and the court refused.

First: Try to End the Case

Lowe's moved for summary judgment. A central defense argument was that Reardon had not disclosed his own medical expert and therefore could not establish medical causation for the injuries and treatment he attributes to the incident.

On July 28, 2026, U.S. District Judge John A. Woodcock Jr. denied Lowe's motion. The court concluded that a factfinder could determine that the Lowe's employee fumbled the wooden fence post, that it struck and damaged Reardon's thumb, and that he sustained at least some compensable injury. The court left for trial the boundary between injuries that may be understood through common sense and more complex medical-causation questions requiring expert testimony.

That ruling did not establish final liability and did not guarantee any damages award. What it did was preserve the case for trial.

Then: Put the Plaintiff's Criminal Record on the Trial List

With summary judgment unsuccessful, Lowe's filed its September 1, 2026 Pre-Trial Memorandum. In that filing, Lowe's listed witnesses it may use to authenticate Reardon's prior criminal convictions and identified copies of those convictions as potential trial exhibits for impeachment under Federal Rule of Evidence 609.

Those convictions do not determine whether a Lowe's employee mishandled the fence post or whether the post struck Reardon's thumb. Lowe's is invoking a separate evidentiary rule aimed at credibility. Whether any particular conviction may actually be shown to the jury is for the court to decide.

Reardon is seeking to exclude or tightly limit that evidence. The dispute creates a sharp contrast in the case: a negligence lawsuit centered on what happened inside a Lowe's checkout area may now include a fight over how much of the plaintiff's unrelated criminal history the jury should be allowed to hear.

A Familiar Corporate Defense Move: Summary Judgment

Lowe's is fully entitled to contest lawsuits against it. But Reardon's case is not the only injury action in which the company has asked a court to dispose of a customer's claim before trial and failed.

Jury verdict
$16.4M+

Hendrickson v. Lowe's

In a 2016 Nevada trial, a jury awarded more than $16.4 million to customer Kelly Hendrickson after finding Lowe's 80% at fault for a fall in water in a garden department. The reported award included medical expenses, pain and suffering, and future pain damages.

Washington Supreme Court • 2025

Galassi v. Lowe's

A customer alleged that improperly shelved wire fencing fell and broke her toe. Lowe's won summary judgment in the trial court, but the Washington Court of Appeals reversed. In 2025, the Washington Supreme Court affirmed that reversal and sent the case back for further proceedings.

Federal court • 2026

Easter v. Lowe's

In Louisiana, a customer alleged that a faucet display board tipped forward and landed on her shoulder. Lowe's sought summary judgment. In April 2026, the federal court denied the motion.

Federal court • 2026

Goldin v. Lowe's

In South Carolina, plaintiffs brought negligence, gross-negligence, and recklessness claims against Lowe's. In March 2026, the federal court denied Lowe's motion for summary judgment.

These cases involve different facts, different states, and different legal standards. They do not prove Lowe's is liable in Reardon's case. They do show something narrower and verifiable: Lowe's has faced significant adverse outcomes in customer-injury litigation, including a multimillion-dollar jury verdict and multiple unsuccessful attempts to obtain summary judgment.

What Happens Next

The central questions remain for trial: whether Lowe's was negligent, what injury the incident caused, and what damages — if any — should be awarded. Lowe's intends to use surveillance video, medical evidence, and orthopedic expert Bruce M. Leslie, M.D. Reardon intends to present his own evidence concerning the incident, his hand injury, and its effect on his ability to perform skilled mechanical work.

A federal jury may ultimately accept Lowe's defense, accept Reardon's case, or reach a result somewhere between the two. But one avenue has already closed: Lowe's did not succeed in ending the entire case through summary judgment.

A customer does not have to be perfect to have a legal right to a fair trial. The jury's job is to decide the Lowe's case on the evidence the judge allows — not to decide whether it approves of the person who filed it.
